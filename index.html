How to use

From stem to kit in four steps.

Made for the way you already work: a song split into stems with Live's stem separation, the drum stem on its own track. Watch the full walkthrough, or follow the steps below.

1

Put Rekit first on the drum stem's track

Drag Rekit from your User Library (Audio Effects › Max Audio Effect) onto the drum stem's audio track. Stems from Live's stem separation work, warped or not. The first time, the button reads Activate: click it and paste the license key from your Gumroad receipt.

Rekit has to be the first device on the track. Nothing before it in the device chain: put any EQ, filter or other effect after Rekit.

2

Click the part you want

In Arrangement view, split or trim out a section (Cmd+E splits at the selection), or take the whole song: what sits between the clip's edges is what gets analyzed. In Session view, it's the clip's loop.

3

Choose where the pads go

Two switches, one choice: everything on one track, kicks on their own tracks, or every pad on its own track. Turning one on turns the other off, and the choice is saved with your set.

Both off: one track

Every sound goes into one Drum Rack on a new track named Rekit, one pad per sound, named by what it is. Closed and open hats keep choking each other.

Kick Track: kicks apart Default

Each kick gets its own track, right below the stem, for processing it separately: a Simpler with that kick's one-shot, playing on C3. Everything else stays in the Rekit Drum Rack.

Every Pad: every sound apart

Every sound gets its own track, kicks first, then the rest. Each is a Simpler on C3 with its one-shot, so a sample you drop on it from your library sounds exactly as it does in the browser.

4

Press Extract

The new tracks appear right below the stem, with clips at the same position and length as the audio clip (or in the same scene, in Session view). While it runs, Extract turns into Cancel.

What you get

The kit, not just the rhythm.

One pad per sound

Kicks, claps, rims, closed and open hats, shakers, percs, rides: however many distinct sounds the stem has, each gets its own pad, named by what it is.

The track's own samples

Every pad is loaded with the one-shot cut from the stem, so the rack sounds like the record from the first play. Replace any of them from your library.

MIDI on Live's grid

Notes land through the clip's warp markers, within about 2 ms of the audio, with velocities taken from how hard each hit plays. The groove survives.

Kicks or every pad on their own track

One switch puts each kick on its own track for separate processing; the other splits every pad out. Each is a bare Simpler on C3, so a sample from your library sounds exactly as it did in the browser.

Stems and an A/B check

Each run also writes a stem per sound, every one-shot, a drums.mid, and a resynthesis of the kit playing the MIDI to compare against the original.

Any length, up to a whole song

From one bar to the full track. Rekit takes its time to get the details right, so expect several minutes; it runs in the background at low priority, and you can keep working in Live.

Rekit vs. Convert Drums

Why not just use Convert Drums to New MIDI Track?

Live's built-in command transcribes a groove into three sounds played by a stock kit. That's a sketch of the rhythm. Rekit rebuilds the kit the track was made with.

Built for electronic drums

In programmed drums every hit of a sound is the same sample. Rekit uses that: it finds the sounds first, then every place each one plays.

Learns from each track

Nothing is preset per genre or per song, and nothing carries over between runs. Drop it on any drum stem; the result depends on that audio alone.

Under the hood

Sounds first, then hits.

Rekit doesn't guess a drum class for each transient. It learns what each sound in the stem looks like over time, then finds every place it plays, even under other sounds.

Runs on your computer. The analysis engine is installed with the device and runs locally at low priority. No uploads, no account, no internet needed.
  1. Find the soundsClear onsets are clustered by their attacks, since identical samples group together. A cluster like "kick + clap on beat 2" is split into its parts.
  2. Learn each sound's shapeEach sound becomes a template of its whole envelope (non-negative matrix factor deconvolution), so a ringing kick explains its own tail, and a hat buried under it still matches.
  3. Keep only real hitsA classifier trained on ground-truth material decides which matches are real. Fragments of other sounds are dropped; a kick split into sub and click is merged back.
  4. Catch what hidesA quiet shaker is found by its pattern on the 16th grid; a ride is found by the extra ring it adds to the hats; filter sweeps on the drum bus are fitted, not mistaken for new sounds.
  5. Cut the kitOne-shots and per-sound stems are separated from the stem, pads named by sound and by role (a broadband hit on 2 and 4 over a four-on-the-floor kick is a clap), and timing refined to about 2 ms.
Accuracy

Measured, not eyeballed.

Scored against ground truth on drum loops and whole songs rendered from Ableton's Core Library one-shots, and checked by ear and spectrogram on real stems.

Good to know

Questions

What do I need?

Ableton Live 12.3 or later with Max for Live (Suite, or Standard with Max for Live), on a Mac with Apple Silicon running macOS 14 or later, or a Windows 10 or 11 PC with a 64-bit Intel or AMD processor. Nothing else: no Python and no other installs. Intel Macs and Windows on ARM aren't supported yet.

Where does Rekit go on the track?

First. Rekit has to be the first device in the drum stem track's device chain, with nothing before it. Put any EQ, filter or other effect after it.

What kind of drums does it work best on?

Programmed, electronic drums, where every hit of a sound is the same sample: house, techno, hip-hop, trap, breaks. Drums played live vary from hit to hit, which Rekit isn't built for.

Do I need a separated drum stem?

Yes. Rekit reads drums only, so give it a drum stem: the drums track from Live's stem separation works well, warped or unwarped, and so does any drums-only audio.

Arrangement or Session view?

Both. In Arrangement view, what sits on the timeline between the clip's edges is analyzed, so split or trim out the part you want first (Cmd+E splits at the selection). In Session view it's the clip's loop, or its start and end markers when Loop is off. The new clip lands at the same position, or in the same scene.

Where do the samples go?

Each run writes its one-shots, per-sound stems, drums.mid and the A/B resynthesis to ~/Music/Rekit/. Use Collect All and Save to copy the samples into your project.

How long does an extraction take?

Several minutes, for a short clip or a whole song. Rekit learns every sound in the stem before it places a single note, and that thoroughness is where the accuracy comes from. The analysis runs in the background at low priority, so Live stays responsive and you can keep working. The status shows its progress, and Cancel stops it at any time.

Can I stop an extraction?

Yes. While it runs, Extract turns into Cancel. During analysis it stops right away; while the tracks are being built, it stops after the current step and keeps what's made.

What doesn't it do well yet?

Sounds that always play together (a ride that only ever sits on a hat, a clap layered on every snare) usually come out as one pad. Pad names can mix up a shaker and a hat. Long trap 808s can re-trigger. And a closed hat, an open hat and a shaker all on the same 16ths is the hardest case.

How does the license work?

Your Gumroad receipt has a license key. The first time you use Rekit, click Activate and paste it in. Each license works on up to 3 computers, in any mix of Mac and Windows. Rekit checks its license online every two weeks and keeps working offline for up to 60 days between checks. Moving to a new computer? Reply to your Gumroad receipt and we'll reset your activations.

How do I uninstall it?

Run sudo "/Library/Application Support/Rekit/uninstall.sh" in Terminal.
